Receiving needs no licence. Transmitting on amateur bands does — and that is the whole distinction to
carry out of this module. There are three US classes, each a written exam (no Morse code at any class)
through a Volunteer Examiner Coordinator:

@ref knowledge/ham-radio/learned/licensing-path-us.md#the-three-classes

The path is straightforward: study the current question pool (it rotates on a three-year cycle),
register an FCC FRN, sit the exam through a VEC session, pay the fee, and your call sign appears in ULS
within days. Licences last ten years and renew without re-examination:

@ref knowledge/ham-radio/learned/licensing-path-us.md#getting-licensed

Here is the part that matters for this device, and the part Track C's page states outright. A
Technician licence covers 2 m and 70 cm in full — exactly where the useful VHF/UHF activity is. But a
licence does **not** give Fancy a transmitter: the SDR is receive-only, and transmitting would need a
separate radio. The licence makes acquiring one *useful*; it legalises nothing currently in use,
because the only transmitter on the build (the SX1262) already runs in licence-free ISM spectrum.

@ref knowledge/ham-radio/learned/licensing-path-us.md#what-technician-gives-this-build

So the honest framing: finishing this track prepares you for the exam, and everything Fancy does on
these bands today is receive-only and needs no licence at all. That receive-only workflow — repeaters,
APRS, satellite downlinks, FT8/WSPR spotting — is complete in itself, and a good way to learn the
bands before you sit the test:

@ref knowledge/ham-radio/learned/licensing-path-us.md#receive-only-participation-today
